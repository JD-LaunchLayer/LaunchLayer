## Quick checks

Try one step at a time.

1. **Another wall socket.** Not the same extension lead. A cheap strip can struggle when a heater or a second laptop is plugged into it.
2. **Reset the power.** Shut the laptop down, unplug the charger, and hold the power button for a few seconds. Plug the charger back in and switch on. This can clear a stuck "not charging" reading, though it won't fix a failed part.
3. **Restart the laptop.** On Windows 11, "plugged in, not charging" sometimes clears after a normal restart.

## Charger and cable

Try a known-good charger that's compatible with your laptop, with the right tip, voltage and wattage. If a second charger fills the laptop straight away, the first charger is the likely fault. When neither works, the port or something inside the laptop becomes more likely.

Look at the light on the brick, if it has one. No light, and a cable that bends flat where it leaves the block, usually points to a failed charger. A cracked strain relief, the rubber collar where the cable meets the brick, is a fire risk as well as a charge fault. Please unplug it and stop using it. It isn't safe to keep charging. Bring it in and we'll make it safe.

A USB-C lead can fail on its own, even when the brick's fine. A frayed cable that still clicks in will drop power as soon as the laptop moves. Swap the lead before you decide the port has failed.

Match the wattage printed on the original brick. On USB-C, a lower-powered charger, like a 30W phone brick on a laptop that wants 65W, usually isn't dangerous. It just can't keep up, so you'll see slow charging, or a laptop that shows the plug but won't charge while you work.

Barrel chargers are a different risk. A universal adapter set to the wrong voltage, or a tip that fits but isn't meant for your model, can damage the charging circuit, the circuitry on the board that controls charging. Stick to the original or a replacement made for your exact model.

## Charging port

If charging cuts in and out when the lead moves during normal use, stop moving it and have the cable and socket checked. Rocking the plug because it still works when you rest the lead on a book is how the jack tears the pads off the board inside.

Charging at one angle and not another points to the port. It charges flat on the table and stops when the lead hangs off a sofa. It charges with the plug pushed up, and not when it sits straight. That usually means the DC jack or USB-C port rather than the battery, and it's rarely a reason to replace the laptop.

How the port is fitted depends on the model. On some laptops the charge socket is removable or sits on a small board of its own. On others it's soldered straight to the main board, which means careful soldering work. We'll tell you which yours has on the free diagnostic. Barrel jacks on older HP, Dell and Lenovo machines fail in a similar way: the plastic cracks, the socket pulls, and the pads lift.

If the case is also twisted, tell us, because a lid that moves when you plug in can mean a hinge fault as well as a jack.

Two good chargers and a firm plug, and still no plug icon or sign of power, can point to the charging circuit inside the laptop. We check that on the free diagnostic before we quote.

Liquid near the port is a different problem. Do not keep plugging the charger in. Follow the [spill steps](/blog/spilled-water-on-laptop-fix-essex/) and tell us when you book.

| What you see | What it usually is |

## Battery and Windows charge settings

These checks are for a laptop that runs on the charger and shows the plug icon. Power is getting in, so the question is whether the battery will fill and hold it.

Windows 11 can show the lead as connected and still say "not charging". The percentage can also sit at the same number all day, often around 80%.

On many Dell, Lenovo and HP laptops that stop is on purpose. A charge limit keeps the battery from filling all the way so it lasts longer. Turn the limit off in the manufacturer's app if you want a full charge. It's often called conservation mode or battery-health mode, and it's a setting, not a broken battery.

For a clearer picture, open Command Prompt and type `powercfg /batteryreport`, then open the file it saves. The report compares the battery's original capacity with what it holds now.

A worn battery shows up as a percentage that falls from a lot to nothing in a few minutes, a laptop that switches off without warning on battery, or one that dies the moment you unplug a charger you trust.

If the laptop runs on the charger, the report looks healthy, and it still won't fill, there may be a charging-circuit fault. A new battery won't fix that.

## FAQ

### Why is my laptop plugged in but not charging?

It can be a weak or loose charger, an underpowered USB-C charger, a dead wall socket, a loose port, a Windows charge limit, or a worn battery. Work through the checks above before you buy a part.

### Is it the charger, the port, or the battery?

Try a known-good compatible charger in a different wall socket. If that charges the laptop, the charger or the socket was the likely cause. An icon that only appears at an angle, or flickers when the lead moves, points to the port. A laptop that dies the moment you unplug a charger you trust points to the battery. They're strong clues, not proof.
