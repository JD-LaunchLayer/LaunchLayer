If your laptop takes two minutes to reach the desktop, freezes when you open Photos, or chatters away every time you save a file, it's probably not "too old". There's a good chance it's still running a spinning hard drive.

A common example is a perfectly decent i5 or Ryzen machine from 2018–2021, with 8 or 16GB of RAM, and a 5400rpm disk that struggles to keep up with Windows 11. A solid-state drive (SSD) is a drive with no moving parts, and fitting one is usually the biggest speed change you can make without replacing the laptop. It's worth trying before you shop for a new machine.

An SSD upgrade is a different job from fixing a laptop that [won't turn on](/blog/laptop-not-turning-on-slow-fix/). A slow machine isn't automatically a reason to [buy new](/blog/laptop-repair-vs-replace-sustainability-essex/). It's a parts swap that moves your files across.

## Is it the hard drive, or is Windows just being slow?

Three clues tend to show up together:

1. **The drive chatters** when you copy a folder or open Outlook. SSDs are silent, while a mechanical disk has to move its heads to find each file.
2. **In Task Manager, open Performance, then Disk.** If Disk sits at 100% while the processor is almost idle, the disk is likely the bottleneck. That alone doesn't mean a virus.
3. **It was fast in 2019 and miserable in 2026** with the same apps. Windows, Chrome and Teams have grown, and the spinning disk hasn't.

Heat can also make a machine feel slow. If the fans roar and it only crawls after twenty minutes, read the [overheating guide](/blog/laptop-fan-noise-fix-essex/) first, because an SSD won't fix a laptop that's slowing itself down to stay cool.

A machine that takes five minutes to boot and clicks or grinds while it does can have a failing disk, not just a slow one. Stop using it and treat it as a [data recovery](/data-recovery/) problem. We'll assess the drive before any copy is attempted, and explain the costs before work starts.

## What does an SSD upgrade involve?

The usual job keeps the Windows you already have. We move the existing drive across, files, programs, licence, desktop clutter and all, unless you ask for a fresh Windows setup.

1. Check the old disk's health first, so a failing drive isn't copied onto a new one without you knowing.
2. Copy your existing drive.
3. Fit a 2.5" SATA SSD or an M.2 NVMe module, whichever the laptop can take.
4. Put that copy on the new drive, give it the free space, and check that it boots.

How long it takes depends on the laptop and the part, and we agree the timing with you before we start. You keep your PIN, your Outlook profile, and that folder of PDFs you couldn't recreate.

## SATA or NVMe: which drive will the laptop take?

The right SSD depends on the exact laptop model and its storage connections. We check compatibility before quoting for a drive.

The motherboard decides the fastest drive it can use. A Gen5 stick in a Gen3 slot only runs at Gen3 speed, so we fit a drive that matches the slot you have. If you're building a new PC rather than upgrading a laptop, the [custom PC page](/custom-pc-builds/) is the cleaner route.

Capacity is the other honest conversation. 256GB felt huge in 2016, but in 2026 it fills up with Teams caches and phone backups. 500GB is a sensible floor for a home laptop, and 1TB makes sense if you keep raw photos or a Steam library on the machine.

No. The laptop decides whether it takes a 2.5" SATA drive or an M.2 NVMe drive, and how fast that slot can run. We match the drive to the slot.

### Do I need a new laptop instead?

Not if the rest of the machine is still fine and the slow part is the spinning disk. A cracked screen or a laptop that won't turn on is a different job, and we'll tell you before you pay for a drive.
