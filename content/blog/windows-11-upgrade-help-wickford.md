Good news first: a message saying your PC "can't run Windows 11" doesn't mean it's broken. The compatibility checker has spotted something it wants changed. Sometimes that's a setting we can switch on, and sometimes a small upgrade helps. Either way, there's a sensible way forward.

If you're in Wickford and your PC says "this processor isn't supported" or "TPM 2.0 must be enabled" (TPM is a small security chip inside your PC), you're not alone. ## What the checker is flagging

- **TPM 2.0.** The security chip we mentioned. On many PCs from 2018–2020 it's there but switched off in the firmware settings (the PC's built-in start-up menu), where it may be called "PTT" or "fTPM". If it's there, we can switch it on.
- **Secure Boot.** A start-up safety setting that checks the software starting up is trusted. It's sometimes left off after a reinstall, and we can usually switch it back on.
- **RAM.** Your PC's short-term memory. You need at least 4GB, so the checker flags anything lower. 8GB is the practical minimum in 2026, and 16GB helps if you keep lots of tabs open.
- **The processor list.** Your processor (the PC's brain) usually can't be upgraded without a new main board. A good i5-6500 or early Ryzen machine may never be on Microsoft's list. Workarounds exist, and for a home PC we'll explain the pros and cons first. If you use it for work email, we'll talk through what's safest.

## Options, in order

1. **Enable TPM or Secure Boot and upgrade in place** (install over your current setup). Best when the hardware is fine.
2. **SSD and RAM, then upgrade.** Best when the hardware is fine but tired.

## Licences, files, and buying Windows again

Most home PCs already have a digital licence (proof your copy of Windows is genuine) tied to the motherboard, the main circuit board. An upgrade in place keeps it, and a clean install (a fresh Windows) on the same board usually activates online.
